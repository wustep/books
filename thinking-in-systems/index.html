THE BUILDING BLOCKS

Stocks & flows

A stock is what has accumulated. A flow is how it changes.

Water in a reservoir is a stock, measured in liters. Water arriving or leaving is a flow, measured in liters per minute. A stock carries the history of those flows: turning off the tap doesn’t instantly empty the reservoir.

The same lens works for unread messages, skills, or trust. Ask what is building up, then look for what adds to it and what drains it. A stock stays steady when total inflow equals total outflow—even while plenty is moving.

THE ENGINES OF CHANGE

Feedback loops

Sometimes the result circles back to shape what happens next.

A reinforcing loop amplifies a change. More practice builds skill; greater skill can make practice more rewarding. These loops can also accelerate decline: less trust, less cooperation, even less trust.

A balancing loop responds to a gap. A thermostat compares room temperature with a setting and adjusts heating. It tends toward a target, though delays and weak responses can keep it from settling there.

FOLLOW THE LOOP
More skill↓R↑More rewarding practice

Change feeds further change.

Illustrative model: a stock grows by 5.5% of its current size each step, with no resource limit.

↳

Reinforcing and balancing describe behavior, not goodness. Growth can be harmful; stability can preserve an unfair situation. Real systems contain many interacting loops.

THE SPACE BETWEEN

Delays

The world takes time to answer.

A decision and its consequences rarely arrive together. Trees take years to mature; hiring takes weeks; information takes time to travel. If we react to yesterday’s conditions, we may keep pushing after enough has already been done.

That can create overshoot and oscillation: too much correction, followed by too much correction in the other direction. Watch what happens when a controller adjusts a stock using an increasingly old reading.

CHANGE THE WAITA delayed balancing loop
Current information helps the stock approach its target smoothly.

Each step corrects 12% of the perceived gap to a target of 60. The stock starts at 20 and is bounded between 0 and 100. Only the age of the reading changes.

Same response strength. Different timing.

↳

A practical move: Match the pace of your decisions to the system’s response time. Track actions already in progress, and allow their effects to become visible.

WHERE TO INTERVENE

Leverage points

The place you push matters as much as how hard you push.

Meadows arranged twelve places to intervene, from adjusting numbers to reconsidering the beliefs that organize a system. Deeper interventions can reshape many behaviors at once.

This is a guide to inquiry, not a guaranteed ranking for every situation. Context, direction, and feasibility matter. Explore the ladder through one example: a community library.

12 PLACES TO INTERVENEIncreasing potential for change ↓
12Numbers & parametersTUNE THE SETTINGS+

Adjust a quantity. Library: extend loan periods from two weeks to three.

11Buffers+

Change reserves relative to demand. Library: hold spare copies of popular books.

10Physical structure+

Rearrange stocks and pathways. Library: add a neighborhood branch or a bookmobile.

09Delays+

Change response times. Library: shorten the wait between a request and a purchase.

08Balancing feedbackRESHAPE THE CONNECTIONS+

Strengthen correction. Library: use growing waitlists to trigger extra purchases.

07Reinforcing feedback+

Alter amplification. Library: help successful reading groups recruit and support new groups.

06Information flows+

Reveal missing information. Library: publish unmet requests, including those from underserved neighborhoods.

05Rules+

Change permissions and incentives. Library: remove overdue fines that prevent people from borrowing.

04Self-organizationREIMAGINE THE SYSTEM+

Enable new structures. Library: let residents create and run their own lending programs.

03Goals+

Redefine success. Library: prioritize equitable learning over the number of loans.

02Paradigms+

Question foundational beliefs. Library: see knowledge as a shared resource rather than a scarce privilege.

01Freedom from paradigms+

Hold every worldview provisionally. Library: stay open to entirely different ways of supporting learning.

Order adapted from Meadows’ Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System ↗. Library examples are original illustrations, not claims about particular policies.

05

PATTERNS TO RECOGNIZE

System traps

New characters. Familiar plot.

Different systems can produce surprisingly similar trouble. These recurring structures are often called archetypes. Seeing the pattern helps us examine incentives and relationships instead of simply blaming the people involved.

Meadows describes eight traps and opportunities. Open each field note for an everyday example and a possible way to change the pattern.

⇄

Policy resistance

Competing goals cancel out change.

+

A team adds approvals to reduce errors; colleagues invent workarounds to meet deadlines.

A way out: Bring the parties together to design for both speed and quality.

◎

Tragedy of the commons

Private gains, shared depletion.

+

Everyone books extra time in a shared studio, leaving it unavailable when others need it.

A way out: Agree on fair access, visible usage, and shared stewardship.

↘

Drift to low performance

Lower results become the new normal.

+

A service misses its response target, then keeps weakening the target to match its results.

A way out: Anchor standards to purpose and learn from better performance.

⇈

Escalation

Each side’s defense fuels the other.

+

Two cafés repeatedly undercut each other’s prices until neither can cover its costs.

A way out: Stop matching every move; compete on distinct strengths.

↗

Success to the successful

Advantage earns more advantage.

+

The busiest club gets the most funding, leaving newer clubs little chance to attract members.

A way out: Provide entry support and multiple paths to resources.

⟳

Shifting the burden

Relief displaces lasting capacity.

+

A manager rescues every difficult project; the team never gets to develop its own judgment.

A way out: Pair temporary help with practice, ownership, and a plan to reduce dependence.

⌁

Rule beating

The letter of a rule defeats its intent.

+

A support team closes tickets prematurely to satisfy a resolution quota.

A way out: Redesign the rule around whether customers’ problems are actually resolved.

⊙

Seeking the wrong goal

The metric replaces the purpose.

+

A learning app maximizes time on screen, even when shorter sessions teach more.

A way out: Evaluate learning and retention, and keep questioning what counts as success.

↳

Use patterns as questions, not diagnoses. A real situation may contain several traps. Listen to the people inside it before deciding what needs to change.

FROM KNOWING TO LIVING

Dancing with systems

Understanding is an invitation to participate more wisely.

Systems thinking doesn’t give us perfect prediction or control. Meadows’ closing guidance asks for a different posture: observe carefully, learn in public, and remain accountable for what our actions set in motion.

These six practices gather some of that guidance into everyday starting points. They are an invitation to keep learning, not a complete list of her principles.

01 / OBSERVE

Learn the rhythm first.

Watch behavior over time before intervening. Ask what the system already does well.

02 / STAY CURIOUS

Make your assumptions visible.

Share your model, invite corrections, and treat being surprised as useful information.

03 / KEEP LISTENING

Let information travel.

Protect honest feedback. Put consequences within sight of those making decisions.

04 / WIDEN THE VIEW

Care beyond the boundary.

Consider distant places, future people, and effects outside your department or discipline.

05 / HONOR THE WHOLE

Notice what numbers miss.

Make room for trust, dignity, resilience, and other qualities that resist easy measurement.

06 / KEEP ADAPTING

Hold purpose, revise the path.

Experiment, watch the response, and adjust your actions while staying grounded in your values.

TAKE IT INTO YOUR WORLD

A small practice for today.

Choose something that keeps happening in your life. What relationships might be producing the pattern?
