WHAT MAKES A SYSTEM?

Parts, connections, and a function or purpose. A queue is more than a pile of requests. Arrival rates, working rules, and the reason for doing the work shape how the pile changes. Watch behavior over time to see how the parts work together.

01

FIELD NOTE / STOCKS & FLOWS

First, name what builds up.

A stock is an accumulation at a moment in time. A flow changes it over time. Water in a tank is measured in liters. Water entering or leaving is measured in liters per minute.

A backlog is also a stock. Requests arrive; completed work leaves. Cutting arrivals helps, but the backlog still grows if requests arrive faster than you finish them. A steady stock can have large, equal flows moving through it.

Stock now + Inflow over time − Outflow over time = Stock later

02

FIELD NOTE / FEEDBACK

Follow the effect back to its cause.

A reinforcing loop amplifies change. Rushing can cause mistakes. Mistakes create rework. Rework increases the pressure to rush. The effect returns to strengthen its cause. Reinforcement can accelerate growth or decline.

A balancing loop counters a gap. A team sees a backlog above its target and shifts time toward completing requests. The response reduces the gap. “Balancing” describes what the loop does; it does not mean the target is desirable.

Illustrative model: a stock grows by 5.5% of its current size each step, with no resource limit.

IN THE FIELD

Draw a closed loop. Choose one consequence of your action. Does it return to strengthen the original change or counteract it?

03

FIELD NOTE / DELAYS

The response may still be on its way.

A delay is a wait between cause and effect. Actions take time to change a stock. Information about the result can arrive later still. Repeated corrections based on old readings can push a stock past its target, then back the other way.

Imagine adding staff whenever the backlog looks too high. Hiring and training take time. If you ignore people already being trained, you may keep adding capacity you will not need.

04
MODEL 03 / DELAYS A delayed balancing loop
Current information helps this controller approach its target smoothly.

The controller adjusts using an older reading of the stock.

Model assumptions +

Each step adds 12% of the gap between the target of 60 and the reading available to the controller. The stock starts at 20 and is bounded between 0 and 100. Delays are whole steps; only the age of the reading changes.

Increase the delay from 0 to 10. Watch the stock cross its target and change direction. This is a teaching model, not a prediction.

IN THE FIELD

Before adjusting again: How long should the last change take to work? What is already in progress but missing from today’s measurement?

04

FIELD NOTE / RESILIENCE

Keep the ability to recover.

Resilience is the capacity to absorb a disturbance and keep functioning. A quiet system is not necessarily resilient. It may simply have avoided a serious test. Recovery depends on the responses available when conditions change.

A team scheduled to 100% capacity can be efficient on an ordinary day and brittle when someone is absent. Slack, several ways to do the work, and people who can adapt give it room to respond.

A sketch of a tradeoff, not a staffing forecast. Spare time is one buffer. Shared skills and alternate routes matter too.

Three things to distinguish +

Resilience: recover after a disturbance.

Self-organization: create or revise structures.

Hierarchy: organize smaller systems within larger ones. Coordination helps when the larger level supports the work below it.

IN THE FIELD

Test a disturbance. If the busiest person were absent for a week, what would stop? Which reserve, alternative route, or shared skill would help?

05

FIELD NOTE / LEVERAGE

Change what shapes the behavior.

You can adjust a number without changing the pattern. Longer library loan periods are one intervention. Changing who can borrow, who sees unmet demand, or what the library is trying to achieve reaches further into its structure.

Meadows orders twelve kinds of intervention from parameters toward underlying beliefs. Use the list to widen your options. A deeper change is not automatically feasible or effective in a particular situation.

TWELVE PLACES TO LOOK From settings to assumptions ↓

12–09 / Stocks and timing

12 Numbers & parameters TUNE THE SETTINGS +

Adjust a quantity. Library: extend loan periods from two weeks to three. Watch: longer loans also delay access for the next reader.

11 Buffers +

Change reserves relative to demand. Library: hold spare copies of popular books. Watch: reserves use space and money even when demand is low.

10 Physical structure +

Rearrange stocks and pathways. Library: add a neighborhood branch or a bookmobile. Ask: which readers cannot reach the current collection?

09 Delays +

Change response times. Library: shorten the wait between a request and a purchase. Watch: a faster response still needs a useful demand signal.

08–05 / Feedback, information, rules

08 Balancing feedback RESHAPE THE CONNECTIONS +

Strengthen correction. Library: use growing waitlists to trigger extra purchases. Ask: does the response keep up with the size of the gap?

07 Reinforcing feedback +

Alter amplification. Library: help reading groups recruit and support new groups. Watch: attention can concentrate in groups that already have it.

06 Information flows +

Reveal missing information. Library: make unmet requests visible to purchasing staff. Ask: whose requests never enter the records?

05 Rules +

Change permissions and incentives. Library: remove overdue fines that prevent people from borrowing. Watch both access and the return of shared books.

04–01 / Organization, goals, beliefs

04 Self-organization REIMAGINE THE SYSTEM +

Enable new structures. Library: let residents create and run their own lending programs. Ask: do they have the resources as well as permission?

03 Goals +

Redefine success. Library: prioritize access to learning over the number of loans. Ask: what decisions would change under that goal?

02 Paradigms +

Question foundational beliefs. Library: treat access to knowledge as a shared responsibility. Ask: which existing rules depend on a different belief?

01 Freedom from paradigms +

Hold every worldview provisionally. Library: stay open to different ways of supporting learning. Ask: what evidence would change the library’s view of its role?

Adapted from Meadows’ essay on leverage points. The grouping and library examples are this guide’s illustrations. They do not describe a specific library.

IN THE FIELD

Compare two interventions. One changes a quantity. Another changes information, a rule, or a goal. Name who could make each change and what behavior it would encourage.

06

FIELD NOTE / SYSTEM TRAPS

Different people. The same pattern.

When a problem survives repeated attempts to fix it, look for the structure that recreates it. Competing goals, shared resources, and incentives can make sensible individual choices produce a poor result together.

Use the symptoms below to choose a pattern to investigate. Each note gives an example, a possible intervention, and a question to check your explanation. The trap names follow the book; the examples are original.

⇄

Policy resistance

Competing goals cancel out change.

+

A team adds approvals to reduce errors; colleagues invent workarounds to meet deadlines.

Try: Bring the parties together to design for both speed and quality.

Check: Which goal makes each workaround sensible to the person using it?

◎

Tragedy of the commons

Private gains, shared depletion.

+

Each team reserves extra time in a shared studio as insurance. Together they leave too little usable time for others.

Try: Agree on fair access, visible usage, and shared stewardship.

Check: Does anyone experience the full cost of using more of the shared resource?

↘

Drift to low performance

Lower results become the new normal.

+

A service misses its response target, then keeps weakening the target to match its results.

Try: Anchor standards to purpose and learn from better performance.

Check: Is the team changing its work, or changing the definition of acceptable work?

⇈

Escalation

Each side’s defense fuels the other.

+

Two cafés repeatedly undercut each other’s prices until neither can cover its costs.

Try: Stop matching every move; compete on distinct strengths.

Check: Is each new move a response to the other side’s last move?

↗

Success to the successful

Advantage earns more advantage.

+

The busiest club gets the most funding, leaving newer clubs little chance to attract members.

Try: Provide entry support and multiple paths to resources.

Check: Does today’s allocation make yesterday’s winners more likely to win again?

⟳

Shifting the burden

Relief displaces lasting capacity.

+

A manager rescues every difficult project; the team never gets to develop its own judgment.

Try: Pair temporary help with practice, ownership, and a plan to reduce dependence.

Check: Is the team becoming more able to act without the rescuer?

⌁

Rule beating

The letter of a rule defeats its intent.

+

A support team closes tickets prematurely to satisfy a resolution quota.

Try: Redesign the rule around whether customers’ problems are actually resolved.

Check: Can the target be met while the original problem remains?

⊙

Seeking the wrong goal

The metric replaces the purpose.

+

A learning app maximizes time on screen, even when shorter sessions teach more.

Try: Evaluate learning and retention, and keep questioning what counts as success.

Check: If this metric improved, could the intended outcome still get worse?

IN THE FIELD

Use a trap as a question. Who benefits from the current pattern? Who pays the cost? Ask the people involved before deciding you have found the explanation.

07

FIELD NOTE / USE THE GUIDE

Put a real problem on paper.

Start with something specific that keeps happening. “Work is stressful” is broad. “Our queue doubles every Friday” gives you a pattern to examine.

These notes are a working explanation. Leave gaps where you do not know. Start with a conversation or a measurement if you lack evidence for a change.

YOUR FIELD NOTES

1 of 4
01 What keeps happening?
02 What accumulates?
03 What comes back? What takes time?
04 What will you test?

Write here, or use these questions in a notebook.

Keep the model open to correction.

Track behavior over time. Ask whose view is missing. Watch what your change does beyond the boundary you drew. Revise the explanation when the evidence disagrees.

For Meadows’ fuller account of working with systems, read Dancing With Systems.

IN THE FIELD

Set a review date. Give the system enough time to respond. Check effects on people outside your original picture, as well as the result you intended.
